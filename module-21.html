Module 2 - Connection to land 

Moving to Canada has allowed me to understand the concept of kinship from a much more personal perspective. In Colombia, I grew up in a culture where family, neighbors, and the community are a huge part of your daily life. Sharing food (especially during Christmas), looking after other kids as if they were your own, having a house full of people—sometimes people we didn't even know—chatting with neighbors, or going to the corner store to buy just what you needed for the day... those things were just natural to us. Even taking the bus as the main way to get around, and especially supporting each other during hard times, are practices that are very common back home.

When I came here to start over, I realized that building a new life also means building new relationships, new support networks, and a sense of belonging. In my case, the Catholic Church has been a fundamental part of my journey during difficult moments. To be honest, the sense of community and fraternity I feel in this place is something I never even felt in my own country while practicing my religion. I’ve learned that community isn’t always about sharing the same culture or language; it’s about creating spaces where people feel heard, valued, and not alone.

This experience has deeply influenced how I see myself as an educator. Often, students walk into the classroom carrying family stories, adaptation processes, cultural differences, or emotional needs that aren't always visible. This is where the human side of our teaching needs to show up more. We have to learn to listen not just with our ears, but with all our senses—to see what’s behind every story, or what the kids who barely speak are trying to express. It's so important that children feel heard and safe, without being afraid to raise their voices. Maybe that way, the ones who feel "anonymous" can finally find a way for their ideas to reach others.
