Module - 7- What is reconciliation?

Before moving to Canada, the word "reconciliation" felt like an abstract concept to me, something I associated broadly with international human rights and social justice. However, stepping into the Canadian context, and specifically into the world of education, completely shifted my perspective. I quickly realized that here, reconciliation is not a theoretical idea; it is a profound, deeply personal, and ongoing necessity tied to the lived experiences of Indigenous Peoples, the painful history of residential schools, and the lingering echoes of colonialism.

As I have listened, studied, and grown in my role, I’ve come to understand that reconciliation doesn’t carry a single, uniform definition. It means different things to different stakeholders. For some, the heart of reconciliation lies in truth-telling, uncovering and acknowledging the unvarnished, painful history of the past. For others, it is about action: rebuilding broken relationships, fiercely respecting Indigenous rights, and ensuring self-determination and equity for communities. Witnessing these diverse perspectives has taught me that reconciliation isn’t a final destination or a box to check; it is a continuous, lifelong journey of learning and unlearning.

To figure out where I fit into this process, I have tried to intentionally lean into the discomfort of learning a history that wasn't originally my own. I have engaged with course readings, participated in professional development, and, most importantly, listened to Indigenous voices. As an educator, I constantly reflect on the immense responsibility we hold. The classroom can either perpetuate old harms or become a space of healing, and I want to ensure my practice always leans toward the latter by creating spaces rooted in genuine respect and accurate, honest histories.

Facing ahead, I know my learning is nowhere near finished. I want to continue reading works by Indigenous authors, listening to community elders, and learning from Indigenous educators. My goal is to move beyond just knowing the history to truly understanding and honoring Indigenous ways of knowing and relating. I want to bring these perspectives into my teaching practice not as an afterthought, but respectfully, authentically, and from the heart, doing my small part to shape a truly inclusive and equitable future for the children I teach.
